## Original response

Treat this as a medical emergency in which antibiotics come before any test that would delay them. The usual target is antibiotics within one hour of arrival.

**First 10 to 15 minutes**

Check airway, breathing and circulation, and get IV access. Draw blood cultures, lactate, a full blood count, CRP, electrolytes, renal function, coagulation and glucose. Start sepsis care. A blood pressure of 105/65 is not frank hypotension, but at 67 with fever and confusion he can deteriorate fast, so give fluids as needed and ask early about ICU or high-dependency care. Look for a rash, a focal deficit and papilledema. Record a GCS, because it drives the imaging decision. Start droplet precautions until meningococcal disease is excluded or he has had 24 hours of effective treatment.

**Antibiotics**

Cultures are drawn first, and antibiotics and dexamethasone are not held back for CT. If a lumbar puncture can be done within minutes, do it. If not, treat first. When the LP is delayed or a CT is needed, blood cultures should be taken and antimicrobial and adjunctive therapy given before the LP or before the patient goes to CT.

For a 67-year-old, the empirical regimen should cover pneumococcus, meningococcus and Listeria. This is ceftriaxone 2 g IV every 12 hours (or cefotaxime), plus ampicillin or amoxicillin 2 g IV every 4 hours for Listeria. Add vancomycin (or rifampicin, in ESCMID) if you are in a setting with penicillin- or cephalosporin-resistant pneumococci. Ceftriaxone does not cover Listeria, which is why ampicillin is added above age 50. The Michigan guideline adds Listeria cover for patients over 50 and advises against piperacillin-tazobactam because of poor CNS penetration. Because he is confused, add IV aciclovir until HSV encephalitis is unlikely (for example, after a negative CSF HSV PCR). Adjust doses for renal function.

Delay matters. Observational data link later antibiotics to higher mortality in adults (Proulx 2005; Køster-Rasmussen 2008).

**Neuroimaging before lumbar puncture**

CT is not needed for every patient. The aim is to avoid missing a mass lesion or brain swelling that could make an LP dangerous. The Hasbun criteria (NEJM 2001) list these indications: age 60 or older, immunocompromise, a history of CNS disease, a seizure in the past week, abnormal consciousness, a focal neurological deficit and papilledema. He is 67, so he meets the age criterion, and his confusion may also qualify depending on his GCS. ESCMID uses a narrower list (GCS below 10, focal deficit, new seizure, papilledema, severe immunocompromise) and does not count age alone. Local policy varies, and CT before LP is the safer choice in an older, confused patient.

Where CT is needed, give antibiotics and dexamethasone first, then scan. The 2023 German guideline recommends the same sequence, with a CSF sample taken directly after imaging if the scan does not show a contraindication. A normal CT does not guarantee that an LP is safe, so watch for falling consciousness, and do not do an LP if there are signs of raised pressure, a bleeding disorder or skin infection at the puncture site.

**CSF analysis**

Send opening pressure, cell count and differential, glucose with a paired serum glucose, protein, lactate, Gram stain and culture. Add a multiplex meningitis/encephalitis PCR, and HSV and VZV PCR given the confusion. Culture and PCR often stay positive for hours after the first antibiotic dose, so earlier treatment is not a reason to skip the LP.

**Dexamethasone**

Give 10 mg IV every 6 hours for 4 days, starting just before or with the first antibiotic dose. In the European trial, this regimen reduced mortality in pneumococcal meningitis, with no benefit seen in other causes, and it can be stopped once pneumococcus is excluded. The benefit comes from earlier dosing, and it is not given if antibiotics were started hours before. Whether to continue it in other causes (for example meningococcal disease) differs a little between guidelines, so follow your local protocol.

**After the first hour**

Narrow the antibiotics on the Gram stain, culture and PCR. Common courses are 10 to 14 days for pneumococcus, 7 days for meningococcus and at least 21 days for Listeria. Monitor neurological status closely, and repeat imaging if he worsens, to look for hydrocephalus, infarction or cerebral oedema. Watch sodium, glucose and seizures. Notify public health if meningococcal disease is confirmed, and consider prophylaxis for close contacts.
